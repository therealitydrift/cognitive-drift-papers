# When Meaning Stops Holding Together

*Why cognition becomes unstable when meaning can no longer hold experience together under accelerating conditions.*

**Cognitive Drift Papers**  
**Jun 21, 2025**

Something feels increasingly unstable in modern cognition, but not in the way we have been taught to look for it. Rates of anxiety, depression, addiction, attentional collapse, and identity instability continue to rise even as neuroscience becomes more precise and technologically sophisticated. We can now map neurotransmitters in real time, identify neural correlates of emotion, and model reward loops with remarkable accuracy. From the outside, it looks like progress. Yet at the level people actually experience their lives, very little feels resolved. What often emerges instead is a diffuse sense of dislocation, a feeling of being unmoored from stable reference points. People do not simply report anxiety or depression. Many describe something harder to name, a sense that reality itself no longer quite adds up. Often the problem is not the absence of information, but the inability to hold that information together in ways that preserve coherence over time.

## Coherence and the Limits of Mechanism

Modern neuroscience excels at studying mechanisms, but it remains less equipped to study coherence itself. Meaning resists clean measurement because it is contextual, distributed, and stretched across time. It does not localize neatly to a single region of the brain or reduce to a single chemical process. As a result, it is often treated as secondary, something that emerges only after enough neural activity has stacked into the right configuration. But this framing may invert the relationship. Meaning is not simply a decorative layer applied after cognition has done its work. It is part of the organizing structure that allows cognition to work at all. Without it, perception does not integrate, emotion loses context, motivation scatters, and identity weakens. The system loses its ability to coordinate itself over time. Many experiences modern psychology treats separately may share overlapping structural pressures beneath the surface, even when their symptoms look radically different.

## Meaning as Compression

At its core, meaning functions as a form of compression. The world delivers far more information than the mind can process raw. Sensory input, emotional signals, social cues, memories, and imagined futures would overwhelm cognition almost immediately if left uncompressed. Meaning is one way the mind reduces that complexity into usable structure through narratives, identities, values, and expectations. These are not decorative beliefs layered on top of reality. They are functional models that reduce complexity into forms the mind can act on. A worldview, a sense of self, and even a hierarchy of what matters all operate as compressed representations that help organize experience. Without them, the mind would be forced to reconstruct reality from scratch at every moment.

What makes these models powerful is that they are recursive. They are not static maps but living structures that are constantly revised as new experience arrives. Healthy cognition depends on these models holding together over time, remaining stable enough to guide action while flexible enough to update without collapsing. When they hold, life feels navigable. When they begin to weaken, everything downstream becomes harder to stabilize because the mind loses confidence in the structures it uses to interpret itself and the world.

## Semantic Fidelity and the Stability of Meaning

Compression alone, however, is not enough. Compression can distort. Fidelity is the constraint that keeps compressed models aligned with reality, preserving enough of the original structure for them to remain useful. What we usually call meaning is often the subjective experience of that alignment. It feels emotional and personal because it is lived from the inside, but structurally it reflects whether the models organizing experience still fit the world well enough to guide action.

When fidelity is high, effort produces expected results, interpretations feel trustworthy, identity feels continuous, and reality feels stable enough to lean on. But when fidelity begins to decay, the change is often subtle. Nothing appears obviously broken. Models still function, but they require more effort to maintain. Interpretation becomes exhausting. Language feels thinner. Institutions begin to feel hollow. Identity feels increasingly provisional. This follows a broader pattern I describe as the Drift Principle. When the volume and velocity of information exceed a system’s ability to compress and stabilize it, fidelity begins to drop. Meaning thins, coherence loosens, and reality feels less stable because the models that once held experience together can no longer settle.

## Why Reward Models Alone Fall Short

Much of modern psychology explains behavior through reward. Dopamine often becomes the central explanatory variable, and motivation, addiction, habit formation, and learning are framed primarily as reinforcement problems. This explains why behaviors repeat, but it does not fully explain why repetition so often fails to satisfy. Reward systems operate within larger structures of meaning. They do not generate those structures themselves.

When meaning weakens, reward mechanisms often compensate rather than repair. Pleasure increases without resolution. Stimulation substitutes for understanding. Relief takes the place of coherence. This may help explain why so many people feel overstimulated yet psychologically undernourished at the same time. The system keeps firing, but it no longer knows what it is converging toward.

## Addiction and Meaning Collapse

Addiction is often described as a hijacked reward system driven by excessive dopamine and runaway feedback loops. But this explanation leaves a deeper question unresolved. Why these behaviors, in these lives, at these moments? People do not become attached to every pleasurable stimulus available to them. They often attach to the ones that temporarily stabilize something missing.

Alcohol does not simply feel good. It can feel regulating. Opiates do not only relieve pain. They can feel like relief from internal fragmentation. Stimulants can feel like direction. Endless scrolling can feel like temporary orientation. What these behaviors often share is not just pleasure, but structural substitution. They offer temporary coherence when stable meaning structures have weakened. Addiction often intensifies when those structures can no longer carry enough of the regulatory burden. The chemical story remains real, but it is often downstream of a deeper structural one.

## What Fidelity Reveals About Chemistry

Seen through this lens, neurotransmitters take on a different role. They do not create meaning. They operate within broader meaning structures. When those structures are coherent, the same chemicals can support clarity, motivation, and convergence. When those structures weaken, the same chemicals can amplify noise, instability, and dispersion.

Dopamine directs attention toward expected value. In stable systems, this helps curiosity converge and effort compound into progress. In unstable systems, dopamine can scatter across too many competing inputs, making everything feel salient without anything fully integrating. Serotonin helps regulate mood, patience, and impulse control, but only when there is enough continuity for regulation to anchor itself to. Without that continuity, emotional flattening can replace genuine stability. Norepinephrine flags uncertainty and mismatch. In healthy conditions, it sharpens attention and resolves. In unstable systems, it remains elevated, creating ambient anxiety without a clear object.

The broader point is simple. Chemistry operates within meaning. It cannot fully substitute for it.

## Why Cognitive Drift Is Accelerating

Human cognition evolved under slower conditions, with more stable communities, bounded environments, and shared narratives. Meaning had time to form, update, and settle through repeated feedback. Modern life removes many of those stabilizing conditions. Information arrives continuously. Contradictions accumulate faster. Institutions shift more rapidly. Identities become increasingly mediated through abstract systems rather than direct experience. The mind continues compressing, but the conditions that once supported stable compression have weakened.

Models now update faster than they can settle. Identity becomes provisional. Interpretation becomes labor. This is where filter fatigue emerges, not simply as overload, but as the gradual exhaustion of deciding what matters when compression systems are forced to operate continuously without resolution. Nothing is dramatically broken. But everything begins to feel subtly off.

These conditions do not affect all minds equally. Different cognitive strategies stabilize meaning in different ways, and some are more vulnerable to overload when informational density rises. Pattern-oriented and highly associative strategies may fragment earlier because they depend on broader contextual integration. More sequential or embodied strategies may remain stable longer, but can become rigid or disengaged under the same conditions. The same drift produces different internal experiences depending on how a mind filters, compresses, and stabilizes reality.

## Why Chemistry Alone Has Limits

This framework does not reject neuroscience or medication. It clarifies their limits. You can tune neurotransmitters, but you cannot chemically rebuild meaning. You can suppress symptoms, but restoring coherence usually requires structure. Meaning reduces the chemical work the brain has to do. Structure narrows the interpretive search space. Purpose extends motivation across time. Rituals and routines pre-compress complexity before chemistry is even required.

Meaning is not simply an abstraction layered on top of brain chemistry. It is part of the condition that allows chemistry to organize itself into something livable. When meaning holds, the system can tolerate stress, uncertainty, and novelty without losing coherence. When it weakens, chemical intervention alone often reaches a ceiling because the problem is no longer purely chemical.

What we may be witnessing now is a gradual erosion of the conditions that once allowed minds to hold together. The signals keep coming. The chemistry keeps firing. But without stable compression, coherence thins. Drift sets in quietly, often long before anyone knows what to call it.
