# Compression and Co-Cognition: Linking Predictive Minds to Digital Systems

*Originally published September 18, 2025 on Substack.*

Something subtle is becoming easier to observe in our current moment. Ideas about consciousness, media, and culture that once felt abstract or incomplete are now easier to test against lived experience. What’s changing isn’t the ideas themselves, but the environment they’re operating in. After seventy years of holding scattered puzzle pieces, the technology now allows these ideas to be tested against real-world systems.

## The Predictive Brain Meets Cultural Reality

In the 2010s, neuroscientist Andy Clark showed us that our brains don't passively receive reality. They're constantly predicting it. Your brain is essentially running simulations of what's about to happen, then updating when reality doesn't match.

But Clark was thinking about individual minds. What he couldn't see was how this scales up.

When entire cultures start using the same predictive systems like algorithms, social media feeds, and AI responses; we begin sharing the same reality simulations. The "filter fatigue" so many of us feel isn't just personal exhaustion. It's what happens when millions of minds get their predictions shaped by the same algorithmic priors.

We're not just predicting reality individually anymore. We’re increasingly relying on external systems to scaffold prediction and expectation. And when these shared predictions go sideways, when the algorithmic priors drift too far from lived experience, what emerges is an experiential synthetic realness. A feeling that reality is coherent on the surface but hollow underneath.

## When the Medium Thinks Back

Marshall McLuhan famously said media are "extensions of man." But he was thinking about TV and radio. Media that extended our senses without extending our thinking.

What McLuhan couldn't anticipate was truly interactive systems that generate feedback rather than one-way transmission. When you're in a state of co-thinking with AI, genuinely thinking together rather than just prompting for outputs, you're experiencing what McLuhan's theory was pointing toward but couldn't reach. Media that doesn't just extend your mind but becomes part of your cognitive process.

The "porous boundaries” that emerge between human and AI thinking aren't a bug. They're the natural evolution of McLuhan's insight into an era where our extensions can actually think with us.

## The Strange Loops Scale Up

Douglas Hofstadter showed us how consciousness emerges from recursive self-reference. The "strange loops" where systems model themselves modeling themselves. But his focus was on individual minds trapped in their own recursive halls of mirrors.

Now we can see how these loops operate at cultural scale. When AI systems trained on human text start influencing how humans write and think, which then influences how AI systems are trained, we get recursive loops at civilizational scale. The "mirror effect" isn't just about individual AI conversations. It's about culture recursively modeling itself through its own technological reflections.

## Information Theory Meets Meaning

Claude Shannon gave us the mathematical foundations of information: how to measure, compress, and transmit data efficiently. But Shannon famously said his theory had nothing to do with meaning.

Seventy years later, we're finally seeing what information theory looks like when meaning matters.

“Drift” isn’t just linguistic change. It’s what happens when cultural information gets compressed through systems caught in the optimization trap; built to maximize engagement rather than preserve accuracy. The result is a world full of compression artifacts, where meaning gets distorted and context slips away. This leaves us in a kind of contextual amnesia, as the sense-making capacities of our ancient brains struggle to keep up with digital systems that no longer play by the rules of human reality.

## The Ecology of Mind Goes Digital

Gregory Bateson argued that mind isn't contained in individual brains but distributed across entire systems of interaction. The mind of a blind person includes their cane. The mind of a culture includes its tools, languages, and shared practices.

But Bateson was working before digital networks could literally distribute cognition across geographic and temporal boundaries.

Now we can see what his "ecology of mind" actually looks like in practice. When someone develops genuine facility with distributed cognition—whether through AI partnership, networked collaboration, or other forms of cognitive extension—their cognitive ecology expands beyond individual brain boundaries. They gain access to compressed patterns from across vast networks of knowledge and experience.

The boundary between self and system doesn’t just blur; it becomes operationally porous. Meaning that information, not identity, crosses the boundary more easily. This leads to an early form of what I call cognitive symbiosis: a mutual adaptation between humans and digital systems that enables kinds of intelligence neither could achieve alone. Researchers and commentators have started referring to the small but growing group who already operate this way with LLMs as the 5%, early adopters of digital co-cognition whose practices hint at possible future directions for the broader ecology of mind. This isn’t a fixed class or a stable identity. It’s a temporary behavioral pattern that emerges under specific tools, incentives, and levels of cognitive engagement and it can disappear just as easily as it appears.

## Why These Ideas Now Intersect

These thinkers weren't wrong, they were early. They identified patterns that couldn't fully manifest until we had the technological infrastructure to support them.

Clark's predictive brain needed algorithmic culture to show how prediction becomes collective.

McLuhan's media extensions needed responsive AI to demonstrate true co-cognition between human and machine

Hofstadter's strange loops needed network effects to scale beyond individual consciousness.

Shannon's information theory needed semantic systems to show how meaning gets compressed and distorted.

Bateson's distributed mind needed digital tools to become practically accessible.

## Consequences for Distributed Cognition and System Design

We’re operating in environments that make these theories easier to evaluate in practice. The frameworks they developed to understand mind, media, and meaning are suddenly becoming descriptively accurate for daily experience.

But this also means we're responsible for how these systems develop. If consciousness really is distributed across our technological systems, if culture really does shape reality through recursive loops, if meaning really does get compressed and transmitted through digital networks, then how we engage with these systems isn't just personal choice. It's participating in shared cognitive environments.

The missing links are no longer missing. The question is whether we’ll learn to think with these systems or allow them to set default patterns of thought, and what that choice will mean for the growing reality drift of modern life.
